    The Crucible
    Acts One and Two

    Page-by-page summaries · Act One: 1102–1120 · Act Two: 1122–1135
    Select a page in the left panel to jump to its summary.

    Page 1102

    Parris prays beside his unresponsive daughter, Betty, and angrily dismisses Tituba when she asks about her. Abigail, his niece, brings in Susanna Walcott. Susanna reports that Doctor Griggs cannot explain Betty’s illness medically and suggests a supernatural cause. Parris insists there is no unnatural cause. The introductory commentary explains how witchcraft accusations can provide an excuse for revenge and settling old grudges.

    Page 1103

    Parris says he has sent for Reverend Hale to investigate, but he and Abigail order Susanna to keep quiet about witchcraft. Abigail warns that rumors have spread through the village. Parris demands an explanation for finding the girls dancing in the forest. Abigail admits dancing and offers to accept punishment, but insists Betty fainted from fright when Parris appeared. Parris suspects Abigail is withholding information.

    Page 1104

    Parris fears his enemies will use the forest incident to remove him as minister. He questions Abigail about Tituba’s behavior, a kettle, and someone he believes was running naked; Abigail denies anything beyond dancing. He also asks why Elizabeth Proctor dismissed her from service. Abigail attacks Elizabeth’s character and insists her own reputation is spotless. Mrs. Ann Putnam arrives and immediately interprets Betty’s condition as supernatural.

    Page 1105

    Mrs. Putnam repeats a rumor that Betty flew, which Parris denies. Thomas Putnam arrives and says their daughter Ruth is also strangely ill. Parris resists calling it witchcraft, while Putnam urges him to recognize the Devil’s involvement. Miller’s background commentary presents Putnam as proud, resentful, and eager to settle grievances, including the rejection of his relative as Salem’s minister and disputes involving the Burroughs family.

    Page 1106

    Miller links Putnam’s bitterness to his later involvement in accusations. Ann Putnam reveals that seven of her babies died and that she sent Ruth to Tituba to learn why. Abigail admits Tituba and Ruth were conjuring spirits but denies participating herself. Mercy Lewis reports Ruth has improved slightly. Putnam urges Parris to announce that he has discovered witchcraft. Parris agrees to address the crowd and pray, while waiting for Hale.

    Page 1107

    After the adults leave, Abigail tells Mercy to admit only to dancing and warns that Parris saw Mercy naked. Mary Warren arrives frightened by the witchcraft rumors and urges them to confess. Betty awakens and reveals that Abigail drank blood as a charm to kill Elizabeth Proctor. Abigail strikes Betty and threatens the girls with violence if they reveal anything beyond dancing and Tituba’s conjuring. Betty becomes motionless again.

    Page 1108

    John Proctor enters, frightening Mary, his servant, who has left home without permission. Miller describes Proctor as independent, respected, and hostile to hypocrisy, but privately burdened by guilt. Mary and Mercy leave. Alone with Proctor, Abigail becomes flirtatious and tells him Betty’s condition began when Parris discovered them dancing. Abigail seeks affection, but Proctor says their relationship is over.

    Page 1109

    Abigail insists she still loves Proctor and believes he loves her. Their conversation reveals a past affair and Abigail’s resentment toward Elizabeth, who dismissed her from the Proctor household. Proctor admits he may still think fondly of Abigail, but firmly refuses to resume the relationship. Abigail rejects his attempt to treat their intimacy as something they must forget.

    Page 1110

    Abigail insults Elizabeth and condemns Salem’s hypocrisy, while Proctor tells her to stop attacking his wife. Hearing a psalm from downstairs, Betty suddenly screams and covers her ears. Parris and the Putnams rush in; Ann Putnam interprets Betty’s reaction to God’s name as evidence of witchcraft. Rebecca Nurse and Giles Corey arrive. Miller introduces the Nurses as widely respected people whose standing and land connections have also stirred resentment.

    Page 1111

    Rebecca quietly calms Betty and suggests the girls’ behavior may pass without intervention. She urges patience, prayer, and caution about searching for evil. Ann Putnam resents Rebecca’s confidence because Rebecca’s children and grandchildren survived while seven of Ann’s babies died. Proctor challenges the decision to summon Hale and complains that Parris’s sermons dwell on punishment. Arguments about religious authority and village divisions grow sharper.

    Page 1112

    Proctor criticizes Parris’s preaching, and Parris complains about his salary, firewood, and lack of respect. Parris also demands ownership of the minister’s house, which Proctor sees as excessive. Parris insists on obedience to church authority and claims a faction is working against him. Proctor responds defiantly, while Rebecca urges reconciliation. The argument reveals that financial disputes and struggles for power underlie the religious conflict.

    Page 1113

    Proctor and Giles exchange friendly jokes about Giles’s frequent lawsuits, then clash with Putnam over timber and disputed property boundaries. Putnam claims land Proctor says he bought from the Nurses; Giles challenges the reliability of Putnam family claims. Reverend Hale arrives. Miller presents him as an enthusiastic specialist who believes his learning can identify witchcraft, while questioning the certainty with which people divide the world into God’s side and the Devil’s.

    Page 1114

    Miller pauses the action to argue that religious and political authorities can use the idea of the Devil to condemn opponents and justify persecution. He compares Salem’s habits of accusation with modern ideological conflicts, including anti-Communist suspicion. He also cautions against dismissing every report as deliberate fraud: Tituba’s confession and the girls’ conduct suggest that some participants believed in, or acted out, the supernatural world they described.

    Page 1115

    Miller continues discussing the connection between fears of sexuality, social repression, and accusations of witchcraft. Hale approaches Salem eagerly, proud that his specialized knowledge may finally prove useful and convinced he is defending goodness. A textbook science sidebar offers ergot poisoning as a possible explanation for some reported symptoms. The sidebar presents a theory, rather than establishing the cause of Salem’s events.

    Page 1116

    Hale arrives carrying heavy books and is welcomed as an authority. He recognizes Rebecca’s reputation and hears about Ruth’s condition and Betty’s supposed attempt to fly. Proctor remains skeptical and leaves, while Giles stays to ask questions. Hale warns that they must investigate carefully and accept his judgment if he finds no evidence of witchcraft. Parris admits discovering the girls dancing, and Ann reveals that she sent Ruth to Tituba to contact her dead children.

    Page 1117

    Rebecca objects to conjuring the dead and leaves, uneasy about the investigation. Giles asks Hale about his wife Martha’s private reading and says he struggled to pray while she was reading. Hale becomes interested, although Giles has not directly accused her of witchcraft. Miller describes Giles as argumentative but fundamentally innocent. Hale begins examining Betty and questioning her about an invisible attacker, while Parris fears the Devil has targeted his household.

    Page 1118

    Hale questions Abigail about the dancing and the kettle in the forest. Parris reports seeing movement in the soup, which Abigail explains as a frog. Abigail shifts blame to Tituba, claiming Tituba made her and Betty drink blood. Tituba says it was chicken blood and denies serving the Devil. Hale questions whether Tituba has sent her spirit against Betty, while Abigail adds that Tituba tried to make her drink the brew and call the Devil.

    Page 1119

    Abigail accuses Tituba of causing corrupt dreams and tempting her away from prayer. Parris threatens to whip Tituba to death, and Putnam demands hanging. Terrified, Tituba begins to confess under pressure. Hale changes to reassurance, telling her she can serve God by exposing the Devil’s agents. The adults press her to identify other people, suggesting names themselves. Tituba’s fear begins turning into relief as confession brings approval and protection.

    Page 1120

    Tituba names Sarah Good and Goody Osburn after the adults press her for names. Ann Putnam treats Osburn’s name as confirmation of her suspicions about her babies’ deaths. Seeing the favorable response, Abigail announces her own confession and starts accusing others. Betty suddenly rises and joins her. Hale and Parris celebrate what they view as deliverance, while Putnam calls for arrests. Act One ends with the girls’ rapidly expanding accusations.

    Act Two · Page 1122

    Act Two opens in the Proctors’ home eight days later. John returns from planting while Elizabeth puts the children to bed and prepares supper. He secretly adds salt to the stew, then compliments her cooking. Their polite conversation about the farm, food, and a possible gift of a heifer shows their efforts to please each other, while the careful atmosphere hints at tension in their marriage.

    Act Two · Page 1123

    John tries to draw closer to Elizabeth, but her restrained response disappoints him. He suggests flowers and a Sunday walk around the farm. Elizabeth asks whether he went to Salem and reveals that Mary Warren disobeyed his orders to attend court. Mary now claims official authority. Elizabeth reports fourteen prisoners and a threat of hanging for those who refuse to confess. Abigail leads the accusing girls, and John recognizes the danger of their growing power.

    Act Two · Page 1124

    Elizabeth urges John to tell the court that Abigail admitted the girls’ behavior had nothing to do with witchcraft. He hesitates because Abigail told him privately and he has no witness. Learning he was alone with Abigail renews Elizabeth’s mistrust. Their argument exposes his guilt over the affair and his resentment at feeling constantly judged. Mary Warren arrives looking ill and exhausted. John angrily confronts her for going to Salem, but her weakness interrupts his fury.

    Act Two · Page 1125

    John rebukes Mary for neglecting her work and leaving Elizabeth without help. Mary gives Elizabeth a small doll she sewed while sitting in court. She reports that the number of accused women has risen to thirty-nine. Exhausted and tearful, Mary says Goody Osburn has been sentenced to hang. John is shocked that the court and Deputy Governor would permit an execution.

    Act Two · Page 1126

    Mary explains that Sarah Good escaped hanging by confessing, while Goody Osburn denied the accusations. Mary describes feeling attacked by Sarah’s spirit and reinterprets Sarah’s earlier begging and muttering as evidence of witchcraft. John repeatedly asks for proof and challenges her claims. Mary defends the court’s judgments and her new importance as an official. She also reports that Sarah Good is pregnant and will be spared for the child’s sake.

    Act Two · Page 1127

    When John threatens to whip Mary, she reveals that Elizabeth was mentioned in court and that Mary defended her. After Mary leaves, Elizabeth believes Abigail wants her executed so she can take her place as John’s wife. John promises to challenge the accusations, but Elizabeth asks him to speak directly to Abigail and destroy any hope of a future together. Their discussion returns to the affair and the meaning Abigail may attach to John’s behavior.

    Act Two · Page 1128

    Elizabeth insists that John must make Abigail understand their relationship is over. John resents the demand, and their argument intensifies until Reverend Hale unexpectedly arrives. Hale says he is visiting on his own to assess households named in court. He has also visited Rebecca Nurse, whose name has been mentioned. John and Elizabeth are astonished that anyone could suspect Rebecca, but Hale insists that even a respected person might secretly serve the Devil.

    Act Two · Page 1129

    Hale questions John’s infrequent church attendance and his decision not to have his youngest child baptized. John explains his distrust of Parris, particularly the minister’s concern with expensive golden candlesticks. Hale treats these choices as weaknesses in the household’s religious record. He then asks John to recite the Ten Commandments. John struggles, repeats one, and forgets the commandment against adultery until Elizabeth supplies it, bringing his private guilt into the examination.

    Act Two · Page 1130

    Hale warns that even a small religious fault can weaken a household. At Elizabeth’s urging, John tells him Abigail admitted the girls were frightened after being caught dancing, rather than bewitched. Hale is startled and asks whether John would testify. John agrees if necessary but questions a court that trusts accusations and coerced confessions. Hale then tests their belief in witches. Elizabeth insists that her upright conduct is incompatible with secretly serving Satan.

    Act Two · Page 1131

    Elizabeth tells Hale to question Abigail rather than her faith. Hale urges the Proctors to attend church and baptize their youngest child. Giles Corey and Francis Nurse arrive with news that their wives have been arrested. Rebecca is accused of murdering Ann Putnam’s babies; Martha Corey is accused after a neighbor connects his dying pigs to her reading. Hale struggles to defend the court despite their distress. Ezekiel Cheever and Marshal Herrick enter on official business.

    Act Two · Page 1132

    Cheever announces that he has a warrant for Elizabeth’s arrest following Abigail’s accusation. He searches for dolls and takes the one Mary brought home. Finding a needle in it, he explains that Abigail screamed during dinner at Parris’s house and a needle was pulled from her belly. Abigail claimed Elizabeth’s spirit stabbed her. John insists Abigail injured herself, but Hale is shaken by what he considers evidence.

    Act Two · Page 1133

    Mary explains that she made the doll in court, inserted the needle herself, and gave it to Elizabeth. Abigail sat beside her and could have seen everything. Hale still questions whether Mary’s memory is reliable. When Elizabeth calls Abigail a murderer, Cheever treats her anger as further evidence. John tears up the arrest warrant and denounces the court’s trust in the accusers. Herrick says he must carry out the arrest, and Elizabeth reluctantly agrees to go.

    Act Two · Page 1134

    Elizabeth tries to remain calm as she leaves, asking John to protect the children from frightening news. John becomes furious when he hears her being chained. Giles demands that Hale act against the fraud, but Hale remains uncertain and suggests hidden sins may explain the crisis. After Hale, Giles, and Francis leave, John orders Mary to accompany him to court and explain the doll and needle. Mary fears accusing Abigail of attempted murder.

    Act Two · Page 1135

    Mary warns that Abigail will expose John’s adultery if he challenges her. John realizes Mary knows about the affair and resolves to risk public disgrace to save Elizabeth. Mary fears that the girls will turn against her and repeatedly refuses to testify. John threatens and physically intimidates her, insisting that his wife must not die for his wrongdoing. Act Two ends with John facing the collapse of his outward respectability while Mary sobs in terror.
